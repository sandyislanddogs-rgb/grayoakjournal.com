How to Nail Your Veterinary Working Interview

November 12, 2026  ·  8 min read

The Short Version

A veterinary working interview is your look behind the curtain, so treat it like the two-way audition it is. Before you say yes, pin down the logistics and whether it’s observation or hands-on. Once you’re there, watch workflow, team dynamics, the type of medicine being practiced, and the overall feel of the place as closely as they’re watching you, and stay curious instead of preachy.

So you got the call. After a resume screen and probably an interview, a hospital has invited you in for a working interview. First things first: pat yourself on the back. They don’t spend a day of their team’s attention on someone they aren’t already interested in. You’ve cleared the early bars, and this is the real one.

Here’s the reframe I want you to carry in the door, because it changes how you’ll behave once you’re there, and it is the most important aspect of this whole thing: this is an investment in your future, not a test you’re hoping to survive. It’s your chance to see a veterinary hospital with its guard down, the way it actually runs on a Tuesday, not the version they’d describe in a glossy interview. A hospital can’t script a working interview. They don’t always know which case is about to walk through the door, which means you get an honest look at how the day flows and, more telling, how they handle it when the day doesn’t cooperate. You are prescreening them as hard as they’re prescreening you.

A quick note on names, because the word gets used loosely. A true “working interview” means you’ll have your hands on patients and actual work. A lot of what hospitals call a working interview is really a shadow day, where you observe and don’t touch. Both are useful, and I’ll use “working interview” as the catch-all here, but knowing which one you’re walking into changes everything from what you wear to whether you get paid. So nail that down first.

One more thing, for the candidate who did not get offered a working interview and it was straight to an offer. If a hospital runs you through its interviews and jumps right to a job offer without one, ask for one anyway. It may not be their norm, and that’s fine, but their habit isn’t your limit. You’re the one deciding whether to hand this place a chunk of your career, and a day on the floor is the best look you’ll get before you do. The worst they say is no, and even the ask tells them you take the fit as seriously as they should.

I wrote the employer’s side of this in the 6-step hiring process post and again in the working-interview piece, employer side. This is the same day from your chair.

Before you say yes: the questions to ask

The worst time to learn you were supposed to bring scrubs is when you’re standing in the parking lot in jeans. Settle the logistics before you agree to a date. None of these are rude to ask. A good hospital will be glad you did, because it tells them you prepare.

Ask the plain who, what, where, when, and the one people skip, why.

  • When, and for how long? Agree on a date and time that genuinely works for both of you, then ask how many hours to expect to be there. A couple of hours and a full shift are different animals, and you may need to arrange your own coverage, childcare, or who’s walking your own fuzzball at noon.
  • Observation or hands-on? This is the big one, and it decides the pay question below. Are you shadowing, or will you actually be expected to work?
  • What should I wear? Scrubs, business casual, closed-toe shoes? Ask. Showing up dressed wrong is a small thing that feels enormous at 8 a.m.
  • Where do I park, and who do I ask for? It sounds trivial. It is not. Wandering in late and lost because you circled the block looking for parking is a bad first thirty seconds, and first thirty seconds count. Know where to put your car and whose name to say at the front desk.
  • For a credentialed role, anything to brush up on? If you’re interviewing for a technician or DVM spot and it’s a true hands-on working interview, ask whether there are procedures they’d like you to be sharp on. There is nothing worse than getting handed a catheter or a dental you’re feeling rusty on, with an audience. Asking isn’t a weakness. It’s professionalism, and it protects the patient.

A word on pay

If it’s observation only, don’t be surprised if it’s unpaid, and don’t hold that against the hospital. You’re being invited to spend a day learning whether you want to work there. That’s worth your time even without a paycheck attached.

If you’re going to be doing actual work, pay should be part of the conversation. Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, when a candidate does productive work the hospital benefits from, that time is generally compensable; genuine observation where you do no work can be unpaid. Some states set stricter rules than the federal floor. So it’s fair to ask, and if you’ll be working, it’s reasonable to expect to be paid for it. This is general information, not legal advice, so check your own state’s wage laws, since they vary.

Once you’re there: what to actually watch for

You walked in to answer one question above all others: do I fit with this team? Everything else serves that. Below is the order I’d run my eyes in, roughly from the easiest to spot to the hardest.

1. Cleanliness. Start with the obvious because it tells you the most with the least effort. Is the hospital clean, or clean-looking only where clients can see? Peek, politely, at the treatment area and the back. A hospital that’s tidy when no one’s watching has standards that hold when no one’s watching, and that’s the kind you want to work in.

2. Efficiency and workflow. Watch the schedule breathe. Does it make sense, or are they stacking appointments so tight that clients stew in the lobby and everyone’s underwater by ten? Then watch the thing a schedule can’t show on paper: how do they handle an emergency or an urgent squeeze? Is a sick walk-in jammed into a day with no give, or do they hold space on purpose for exactly that? How a hospital absorbs the unexpected is the clearest read you’ll get on whether it’s actually organized or just busy.

3. Team dynamics. This is the one you came for, so give it the most attention. Are people pleasant to each other, not just to you? Do they move like a team, covering each other, or like a collection of people who happen to share a building? Watch the small tells: do they know their regular clients by name, do they greet the patients, is there warmth in how they talk to each other when no client is listening. And listen for the opposite. Do they respect their clients and each other, or do you catch trash talk about clients the second a door closes, or little digs at a coworker who stepped away? A team that runs each other down in front of a stranger they’re trying to recruit will do it to you by month two. The person assigned to show you around is a signal too. Someone who’s eager to show you things, who clearly likes where they work, is telling you something real. Someone going through the motions is also telling you something.

4. Do the rules hold? Look for whether people actually follow their own protocols, or whether there’s an official way and a real way. Do they get along in the ordinary give-and-take of a hard day? A hospital where the written standard and the lived standard match is a hospital that will treat you fairly when you’re the one who needs the policy to hold.

How to show them you’re the right hire

Watching them is only half your job. The other half is making it easy for them to picture you on the team. These aren’t tricks. They’re just the difference between a candidate who showed up and one who leaned in.

If you’re observation only, honor that. There are real liability reasons a hospital keeps a shadowing candidate hands-off, and respecting the line is itself a point in your favor. But observation doesn’t mean invisible. You can still show you’d be an asset. Pay attention, look engaged, ask good questions, get into the case with your mind even when you can’t get into it with your hands.

Ask questions that come from curiosity, not from the pulpit. This is where candidates with real knowledge trip themselves. If you see a case handled differently than you would have handled it, the instinct is to show what you know by pointing it out. Resist it. You are a guest observing their hospital, not auditing it. Ask “I’m curious why this one went that way” and you learn something while reading as thoughtful. Say “well, I’d have done X” and you read as the person who’ll be exhausting to work with. Same knowledge, opposite impression. Come from curiosity every time.

Be the person your resume promised. Make sure what you wrote on paper matches who walks through the door. As I said on the employer side, a lot of us use a screening or skills check, an assessment, in other words a test, during this process, and it isn’t there to trip you up. It catches resume padding, yes, but mostly it tells a future employer how much training each candidate will need. If you said you can place an IV catheter, the working interview is where that becomes true or doesn’t. Don’t oversell on paper what you can’t show in the room.

Before you leave: close the loop

Don’t just drift out when the day winds down. Circle back deliberately with two people: whoever you shadowed, and whoever set the interview up.

Ask if they have any questions for you. Ask the questions you still have. And get clear on the single most important thing people forget to nail down: what are the next steps, and when? You should leave knowing what happens now and roughly when you’ll hear.

This is also the point where you can self-select out. If somewhere in that day your gut told you this isn’t the place for you, honor that. You can say so kindly on the spot, or you can take it home and sleep on it, no need to decide in the parking lot. Either way, don’t feel bad about it, and don’t talk yourself into a situation your gut is flagging. You don’t owe anyone a yes, and forcing a fit that isn’t there helps no one, least of all you six months in. Letting a hospital keep a spot warm for a candidate who’s already out the door helps no one either.

That’s the whole point of a working interview, really. It’s your chance to prescreen a job before you commit a chunk of your career to it. Watch everything. Trust what you see when they think no one’s looking. The right hospital will feel like a place you could belong, and the day will tell you that louder than any interview answer could.
